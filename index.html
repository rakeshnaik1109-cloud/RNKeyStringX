Pitch is frequency

A string that swings 440 times a second produces the note musicians call A. Orchestras tune to it, and you will see it written as A4 = 440 Hz. Faster vibration gives a higher pitch, slower vibration a lower one. Human hearing runs from about 20 Hz to about 20,000 Hz; the lowest key of a piano is 27.5 Hz and the highest is 4,186 Hz.

The octave: doubling

Play A at 220 Hz and A at 440 Hz together. They blend so completely that every musical culture gives them the same name. Doubling a frequency raises the pitch by one octave. Halving it lowers the pitch by one octave. The A notes on a piano are therefore 27.5, 55, 110, 220, 440, 880, 1760 and 3520 Hz.

Notice what this means. Pitch is heard as a ratio, never as a difference. From 110 to 220 Hz sounds like the same distance as from 1760 to 3520 Hz, although the second gap is sixteen times wider in hertz.

Twelve steps in an octave

Western music, and the instruments in this book, divide each octave into twelve equal steps called half steps or semitones. Equal here means equal in ratio: each half step multiplies the frequency by the same number. Twelve of them must multiply to 2, so that number is the twelfth root of 2, about 1.0595. This system is called twelve-tone equal temperament.

frequency of a note n half steps above A4 = 440 × 2n/12 Hz

Use a negative n for notes below A4. Two half steps make a whole step. On a guitar one fret is one half step; on a piano the next key, black or white, is one half step.

Timbre: the recipe of harmonics

A real string does not vibrate in one simple way. It vibrates as a whole, in two halves, in three thirds and so on, all at once. The whole-string vibration gives the fundamental, the pitch you name. The others give harmonics (also called overtones) at exactly 2, 3, 4, 5… times the fundamental frequency. Your ear fuses them into one note with a particular colour.

A flute has a strong fundamental and little else. A clarinet favours the odd-numbered harmonics, which gives its hollow sound. A bowed string carries all of them. The mixer below lets you build these recipes and hear them.

The harmonic series also explains why some notes agree with each other. The second harmonic is an octave above the fundamental (ratio 2:1). The third is an octave plus a fifth (3:1), so the step from second to third harmonic is a ratio of 3:2, the interval called a perfect fifth. Ratios 4:3 and 5:4 give the perfect fourth and the major third. Simple ratios sound smooth; you will use them constantly from Chapter 9 onward.

Guitarists can hear harmonics directly. Touch a string lightly, without pressing, exactly above the twelfth fret and pluck: you have forced the string to vibrate in two halves, and you hear the second harmonic alone, one octave up. Above the seventh fret you get the third harmonic.

How a note begins and ends

Timbre is also a matter of time. A piano note starts with a hammer blow and then fades whatever you do. A guitar note starts with a pluck and fades. An organ or a bowed violin can hold a note at full strength for as long as the player wishes. This single difference shapes how each instrument is played, and it is the main reason an electronic keyboard set to an organ sound must be handled differently from a piano. Chapter 22 returns to it.

  • Pitch = frequency. A4 = 440 Hz.
  • An octave doubles the frequency. Twelve equal half steps fill an octave; each multiplies frequency by about 1.0595.
  • Timbre is the mixture of harmonics at 2, 3, 4… times the fundamental, plus the way the sound starts and fades.

Music uses the first seven letters of the alphabet: A B C D E F G. After G comes A again, one octave higher. These seven are the natural notes, and on a piano they are the white keys.

The keyboard is the map

Even if you intend to play only the guitar, learn to read a piano keyboard. It is the clearest picture of the notes that exists, and musicians of every instrument think with it.

Look at the black keys. They come in groups of two and three, all the way up. That pattern is your landmark:

  • C is the white key immediately to the left of every group of two black keys.
  • F is the white key immediately to the left of every group of three black keys.
  • From any C, the white keys going right are C D E F G A B, then the next C.

Half steps and whole steps

The distance from any key to its nearest neighbour, counting black and white keys alike, is a half step. Two half steps make a whole step.

Most pairs of neighbouring white keys have a black key between them, so they are a whole step apart. Two pairs do not: E–F and B–C. Those are half steps. Memorise this now; the whole of scale building in Chapter 7 depends on it.

Sharps and flats

The black keys take their names from their white neighbours.

  • A sharp (♯) raises a note by a half step. The black key to the right of C is C♯.
  • A flat (♭) lowers a note by a half step. The same black key, seen from D, is D♭.
  • A natural (♮) cancels a sharp or flat.

C♯ and D♭ are one key and one sound with two names. Such pairs are called enharmonic. Which name is correct depends on the key you are in, as Chapter 8 explains. The rule applies to white keys too: because E and F are a half step apart, E♯ is the key F, and F♭ is the key E. You will occasionally meet a double sharp (written as a small ×, two half steps up) and a double flat (♭♭, two half steps down).

Three naming systems, one set of notes

Letter names are fixed: A is always 440 Hz or one of its octaves. This book uses them throughout.

Solfège gives each note a singable syllable: Do Re Mi Fa Sol La Ti. In the movable-Do tradition, Do is whatever note the piece is built on, so the syllables describe a note’s job inside a key. That makes solfège the best tool for ear training (Chapter 30).

Sargam, the system of Indian classical music, works the same way: Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni. Sa is chosen to suit the singer or instrument and everything else is measured from it. Re, Ga, Dha and Ni each have a lowered form, called komal; Ma has a raised form, called tivra; Sa and Pa never move. That yields the same twelve notes. If you grew up with sargam, read “Sa” wherever this book says “tonic” or “first degree”.

Octave numbers

To say which C you mean, attach a number. Octaves are numbered from C to the B above it. Middle C is C4, the C nearest the centre of a piano. The A above it is A4, the tuning note. The lowest piano key is A0 and the highest is C8. This book writes every pitch this way: E2 is the lowest string of a guitar, E4 the highest.

  • White keys: A B C D E F G. C sits left of the two black keys, F left of the three.
  • E–F and B–C are half steps. All other neighbouring white keys are a whole step apart.
  • ♯ raises by a half step, ♭ lowers by a half step. Each black key has two names.
  • Middle C is C4.

Tap your foot to any song. The steady tap you fall into is the beat or pulse. Rhythm is everything that happens in relation to it: notes that last one beat, several beats, or a fraction of a beat.

Tempo and the metronome

Tempo is the speed of the beat, measured in beats per minute (bpm). At 60 bpm there is one beat each second. A resting heartbeat is 60 to 80; a march is about 120. Scores also describe tempo with Italian words, listed in Chapter 6.

A metronome clicks the beat for you. It is the most honest teacher you will ever have. Use it from the first week.

Bars and time signatures

Beats are grouped into bars (also called measures), separated on the page by vertical bar lines. The time signature at the start tells you how.

  • The top number says how many beats are in each bar.
  • The bottom number says which note value gets one beat: 4 means a quarter note, 8 an eighth note, 2 a half note.

So 4/4 means four quarter-note beats in a bar, 3/4 means three, and 2/4 means two. In every bar the first beat is the strongest. In 4/4 the third beat carries a lighter stress: ONE two three four. In 3/4, the waltz metre: ONE two three.

Count aloud

For the next four figures, press Play, listen once, then clap or tap along while saying the numbers. The count is printed under the notes. Hold long notes for their full value; your hands stay together until the note ends.

Dividing the beat

Two eighth notes fill one beat. Count them “one and two and three and four and”, written 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &. The numbers fall with the click, the “ands” exactly halfway between. Eighth notes that belong to one beat are joined by a beam.

Notes are written on a staff (or stave) of five lines and four spaces. A notehead sits either on a line, with the line passing through it, or in a space. The higher on the staff, the higher the pitch. Moving from a line to the next space, or from a space to the next line, moves one letter name.

The treble clef

Five lines cannot say which letters they stand for until a clef fixes one of them. The treble clef, or G clef, curls around the second line from the bottom and makes that line the G above middle C (G4). Count from there.

  • Lines, bottom to top: E G B D F. (Every Good Bird Does Fly.)
  • Spaces, bottom to top: F A C E.

The treble clef is used for the right hand of the piano, for the guitar, and for most melodies.

The bass clef

The bass clef, or F clef, has two dots that sit either side of the fourth line and make that line the F below middle C (F3).

  • Lines, bottom to top: G B D F A. (Good Boys Do Fine Always.)
  • Spaces, bottom to top: A C E G. (All Cows Eat Grass.)

It is used for the left hand of the piano, the bass guitar, the cello and other low instruments.

Ledger lines and middle C

Notes above or below the staff are written on short extra lines called ledger lines. The most important is middle C. It sits on one ledger line below the treble staff and on one ledger line above the bass staff. It is the same note, the same key.

The grand staff

Piano music joins a treble staff and a bass staff with a brace. Together they form the grand staff. The right hand usually reads the upper staff and the left hand the lower. Middle C lies exactly between them.

Read by landmarks, then by distance

Mnemonics get you started but they are slow. Fluent readers know a few landmark notes instantly and read everything else by its distance from them.

  • Middle C: one ledger line, between the staves.
  • Treble G: the line the clef curls around.
  • Bass F: the line between the clef’s two dots.
  • High C (C5): third space of the treble staff. Low C (C3): second space of the bass staff.

From a landmark, read steps (line to the next space) and skips (line to the next line, or space to the next space). A skip is a third: on the piano, miss one white key.

Stems, accidentals and other details

  • Stems go up on the right for notes below the middle line and down on the left for notes on or above it. Stem direction has no effect on the sound.
  • A sharp, flat or natural written before a note is an accidental. It applies to that note and to every later note on the same line or space until the end of the bar. The bar line cancels it.
  • Sharps or flats written at the start of every line, just after the clef, form the key signature. They apply to those notes throughout the piece (Chapter 8).
  • Guitar music is written in the treble clef one octave higher than it sounds, marked by a small 8 under the clef. This keeps most guitar notes on the staff.

Dots and ties

A dot after a note adds half of the note’s value. A dotted half note lasts 2 + 1 = 3 beats. A dotted quarter lasts 1 + ½ = 1½ beats and is almost always followed by an eighth note to complete two beats.

A tie is a curved line joining two notes of the same pitch. Play the first and hold it for the combined length; do not strike the second. Ties let a note last across a bar line or across a beat. A curved line over notes of different pitches is a slur, which means play smoothly (Chapter 6).

Sixteenth notes

Four sixteenths fill one beat. Count them “1 e & a, 2 e & a”. The “&” is still exactly halfway. Three patterns are worth learning as words: eighth plus two sixteenths (“1 & a”), two sixteenths plus eighth (“1 e &”), and dotted eighth plus sixteenth (“1 … a”), the skipping rhythm of bar 3 below.

Syncopation

Syncopation places emphasis where the pulse is weak: on an “and”, or on beats 2 and 4, often by tying or holding a note across the strong beat. It is the engine of jazz, funk, Latin and most popular music. The beat does not change; you must feel it more strongly because you are no longer playing on it.

Triplets

A triplet fits three equal notes into the time normally taken by two. Eighth-note triplets put three notes in one beat and are marked with a small 3. Count “1-trip-let, 2-trip-let” or “1-and-a”. They must be even, like a rolling wheel.

Compound time

In simple time (2/4, 3/4, 4/4) each beat divides into two. In compound time each beat divides into three, and the beat itself is a dotted quarter. The commonest compound metre is 6/8: six eighth notes in a bar, felt as two beats of three, ONE-two-three-FOUR-five-six. Jigs, many ballads and “The House of the Rising Sun” are in 6/8. Likewise 9/8 has three such beats and 12/8 has four.

More things you will see

  • Pickup (anacrusis). A piece may begin with an incomplete bar that leads into the first full bar. “Amazing Grace” and “Greensleeves” both start on the last beat of a bar. The final bar is shortened to make up the difference.
  • Swing. In jazz and blues, pairs of eighth notes are played long–short, roughly as the first and last notes of a triplet. It is written as plain eighths with the instruction “Swing”. The blues in Chapter 27 uses it.
  • Cut time (2/2, shown as a C with a vertical stroke). Written like 4/4 but felt as two half-note beats. Used for fast music.
  • Irregular metres. 5/4 is usually felt 3 + 2, 7/8 as 2 + 2 + 3 or 3 + 2 + 2.
  • Tala. Indian classical music organises rhythm in repeating cycles rather than bars. Teental has 16 beats grouped 4 + 4 + 4 + 4, Rupak has 7, Dadra 6. The first beat of the cycle, sam, is the point of arrival, much like the downbeat of a bar.

A scale is a set of notes arranged in order of pitch, from which melodies and chords are drawn. Play the white keys from C to the next C. You already know that sound: Do Re Mi Fa Sol La Ti Do. It is the C major scale.

The pattern

Measure the distance between each pair of neighbours, remembering from Chapter 2 that E–F and B–C are half steps:

C –W– D –W– E –H– F –W– G –W– A –W– B –H– C

Whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. That sequence is the definition of a major scale. Start on any of the twelve notes, follow W W H W W W H, and you have the major scale of that note. The half steps always fall between degrees 3–4 and 7–8.

Building a scale on another note

Take G. Write the letters first, one of each, in order: G A B C D E F G. Now check the steps. G–A whole, A–B whole, B–C half, C–D whole, D–E whole. Then E–F is only a half step, but the pattern demands a whole step. Raise the F to F♯. Finally F♯–G is a half step, as required.

Take F. Letters: F G A B C D E F. F–G whole, G–A whole. A–B is a whole step, but the pattern demands a half. Lower the B to B♭. The rest falls into place: B♭–C whole, C–D whole, D–E whole, E–F half.

  • Use every letter name once, in order. A scale never has both G and G♯, or skips a letter.
  • Adjust with sharps or flats to fit W W H W W W H. A major scale never mixes the two.

The first rule tells you which enharmonic name to use. The fourth note of F major is called B♭ and never A♯, because the letter A is already taken by the third note.

When a piece draws its notes from the G major scale and treats G as the note of rest, we say it is in the key of G major. The order of sharps and flats

Key signatures always add sharps and flats in the same order.

Sharps: F C G D A E B. (Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle.)
Flats: B E A D G C F. The same letters backwards. (Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles’s Father.)

A signature with three sharps is always F♯ C♯ G♯. One with two flats is always B♭ E♭.

Reading the key from the signature

  • Sharp keys: the key is a half step above the last sharp. Last sharp G♯? The key is A major.
  • Flat keys: the key is the second-to-last flat. Signature B♭ E♭ A♭? The key is E♭ major. One flat alone is F major; learn that one by heart.
  • No sharps or flats: C major.

The circle

Start at C and go up five letter names, a perfect fifth: C D E F G. The key of G has one sharp. Up a fifth again: D, two sharps. Each step up a fifth adds one sharp. Going the other way, down a fifth (or up a fourth) from C gives F, with one flat, and each further step adds a flat. After twelve steps in either direction you return to C. Arrange them like a clock face and you have the circle of fifths.

What the circle tells you at a glance:

  • Key signatures. Count steps clockwise from C for sharps, anticlockwise for flats.
  • Closely related keys. Neighbours on the circle differ by a single note, so music moves between them easily.
  • The three main chords of a key. A key’s I, IV and V chords are the key itself and its two neighbours: for C, the neighbours F and G.
  • Enharmonic keys. At the bottom, F♯ major (six sharps) and G♭ major (six flats) are the same keys on the instrument.

Transposing

To transpose is to move a piece to a different key, keeping every distance the same. Singers do it to fit their range; guitarists do it with a capo; keyboard players press a button. On paper, the surest method is to think in scale degrees. “Mary Had a Little Lamb” is 3 2 1 2 3 3 3 in any key. In C that is E D C D E E E. In G it is B A G A B B B. In D it is F♯ E D E F♯ F♯ F♯.

Inverting intervals

Move the lower note up an octave and the interval is inverted. C–E (major third) becomes E–C (minor sixth). Two rules cover every case:

  • The numbers add up to 9: 2nd ↔ 7th, 3rd ↔ 6th, 4th ↔ 5th.
  • Major ↔ minor, augmented ↔ diminished, perfect stays perfect.

Consonance and dissonance

Unisons, octaves, fifths and fourths are the perfect consonances, with the simplest frequency ratios (Chapter 1). Thirds and sixths are the imperfect consonances: sweet, and the main material of harmony. Seconds, sevenths and the tritone are dissonances. Dissonance is not a fault. It is tension, and music lives on the cycle of tension and release.

Intervals larger than an octave are compound: a ninth is an octave plus a second, a tenth an octave plus a third, and so on. You will meet them in chord names such as “add9”.

The natural minor scale

Relative and parallel keys

A minor and C major share all seven notes and the same key signature. They are relative keys. Every major key has a relative minor that starts on its sixth degree, three half steps below the tonic. Going the other way, the relative major starts on the third degree of the minor scale.

A minor and A major share a tonic but differ in three notes. They are parallel keys. Composers move between parallel keys to change mood without changing home.

A key signature therefore names two possible keys. To decide between them, look at where the music rests, especially its last note and chord, and look for the raised seventh described next.

The harmonic minor scale

In natural minor the seventh degree lies a whole step below the tonic, so it has no strong pull upward, and the chord built on the fifth degree is minor and weak. Raise the seventh by a half step and both problems are solved. The result is the harmonic minor: A B C D E F G♯ A.

The raised seventh is written as an accidental each time; it is never part of the key signature. It also creates a gap of three half steps between degrees 6 and 7 (F to G♯), an augmented second, which gives the scale its distinctive colour.

The melodic minor scale

Singers found that gap awkward, so in melodies moving upward the sixth is raised as well: A B C D E F♯ G♯ A. This is the melodic minor. In classical practice the scale comes down as natural minor, since the upward pull is no longer needed. Jazz musicians use the ascending form in both directions.

Inversions

The notes of a chord can be stacked in any order. What matters for the name is which note is at the bottom.

  • Root position: the root is lowest. C–E–G.
  • First inversion: the third is lowest. E–G–C. Written C/E, “C over E”.
  • Second inversion: the fifth is lowest. G–C–E. Written C/G.

Inversions exist for two reasons. They let the bass line move smoothly instead of leaping from root to root, and they let your hand move from one chord to the next with the least motion. A pianist who plays C–F–G always in root position jumps about the keyboard; one who plays C–E–G, C–F–A, B–D–G barely moves. On the guitar most chord shapes already contain doubled notes and inversions; you will see this in Chapter 25.

To find the root of an inverted chord, rearrange its notes until they stack in thirds (every other letter). G–C–E rearranges to C–E–G, so the root is C.

Voicing and texture

The way a chord’s notes are spaced and doubled is its voicing. Notes within one octave make a close voicing; notes spread wider make an open voicing. The way the notes are released in time is the texture: all together as a block chord, or one at a time as a broken chord or arpeggio.

  • Subdominant (IV, and ii): departure. Motion away from home.
  • Dominant (V, and vii°): tension. It contains the leading tone and demands a return to I.

The most common sentence in tonal music is tonic → subdominant → dominant → tonic. The chords I, IV and V are called the primary chords; between them they contain every note of the scale, so they can harmonise any melody in the key.

The twelve-bar blues

The blues is a fixed form of twelve bars using I, IV and V, usually as seventh chords (Chapter 13): four bars of I; two of IV, two of I; then V, IV, I, and a last bar of V to turn the form around. Memorise it as a shape. You will play it on the guitar in Chapter 27 and improvise over it in Chapter 31.

Chords in minor keys

Harmonising the natural minor scale gives i, ii°, III, iv, v, VI, VII. In A minor: Am, B°, C, Dm, Em, F, G. In practice the fifth chord is nearly always made major by borrowing the raised seventh from the harmonic minor: E instead of Em, giving a real dominant. Minor-key songs therefore mix both: Am–G–F–E, the “Andalusian cadence”, uses G natural in the G chord and G♯ in the E chord.

Changing key

To transpose a progression, keep the numerals and change the scale. I–V–vi–IV is C G Am F in C, G D Em C in G, and A E F♯m D in A. Guitarists in particular should learn to think this way, because a capo or a barre moves every chord by the same amount.

  • In any major key: I, IV, V are major; ii, iii, vi are minor; vii° is diminished.
  • Tonic rests, subdominant departs, dominant pulls home.
  • Learn progressions as numerals, then play them in several keys.

Why V7 pulls so hard

G7 is G B D F. Its third and seventh, B and F, are a tritone apart, the most unstable interval there is. B is the leading tone of C and wants to rise to C. F is the fourth degree and wants to fall to E. When both move, the tritone resolves into C and E, the heart of the C chord. This two-voice motion is the engine of tonal harmony.

Leaving notes out

You do not have to play every note of a large chord. The third and seventh define its quality. The root is usually covered by the bass. The fifth adds little and is the first note to drop. A shell voicing of root, third and seventh, or just third and seventh over a bass player, is how jazz pianists and guitarists comp, and it makes smooth chord changes easy: in ii–V–I, the seventh of each chord falls a step to become the third of the next.

Pentatonic scales

Remove the two notes of the major scale that form half steps, the fourth and the seventh, and five remain: 1 2 3 5 6. This is the major pentatonic scale. In C: C D E G A. With no half steps it has no harsh clashes, which is why folk music across the world, from China and Scotland to West Africa and the Andes, is built on it.

Start the same five notes from A and you have the minor pentatonic: 1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7. In A: A C D E G. It is the first scale every rock and blues guitarist learns. The five black keys of the piano form a pentatonic scale too; improvise on them alone and nothing sounds wrong.

The blues scale

Add one note to the minor pentatonic, the lowered fifth, and you have the blues scale: 1 ♭3 4 ♭5 5 ♭7. In A: A C D E♭ E G. The added note and the lowered third are the blue notes. Blues musicians play them over major chords and bend or slide them, so that they sit between the keys of a piano. The friction is the style.

Two symmetrical scales

The chromatic scale is all twelve notes in half steps. The whole-tone scale has six notes a whole step apart (C D E F♯ G♯ A♯); with no half steps and no perfect fifth it sounds dreamlike and rootless. Debussy made it famous.

What makes a melody

A good melody balances three kinds of motion: steps (to the next scale note), leaps (anything larger), and repeated notes. Most tunes move mainly by step, with a few leaps for character. After a large leap, a melody usually turns and moves by step in the opposite direction.

  • Contour. The overall shape: an arch, a wave, a climb. Most phrases rise to one high point and fall away.
  • Motif. A short idea of a few notes with a clear rhythm. Melodies grow by repeating a motif, repeating it higher or lower (a sequence), or varying it.
  • Phrase. A musical sentence, typically four bars, ending with a point of rest. Phrases often come in pairs: a question that ends in the air and an answer that ends at home.

Look back at “Ode to Joy”. It is almost entirely stepwise. Bars 1–4 end on the second degree, a question. Bars 5–8 repeat the opening and end on the tonic, the answer.

Notes that are not in the chord

Melodies do not stay on chord tones. The notes between them have names, and knowing them lets you tell an intended dissonance from a mistake.

  • Passing tone: fills the step between two chord tones. C–D–E over a C chord.
  • Neighbour tone: steps away from a chord tone and straight back. E–F–E.
  • Suspension: a note held over from the previous chord, clashing with the new one before it falls a step into place. The sus4 chord is this, frozen.
  • Appoggiatura: a non-chord note on a strong beat, leaned on, then resolved by step. Very expressive.
  • Anticipation: a note of the next chord arriving early.

Voice leading

Think of a chord progression as several singers, each with a line. Voice leading is the craft of making each line smooth. The working rules:

  1. If two chords share a note, keep it in the same voice.
  2. Move the other voices to the nearest note of the new chord, by step if possible.
  3. Prefer contrary motion between the bass and the upper voices: when the bass rises, let the upper notes fall.
  4. Resolve tendency tones: the leading tone rises to the tonic; the seventh of a chord falls a step.
  5. Avoid moving two voices in parallel perfect fifths or octaves. They lose their independence and the texture thins.

Pianists apply these rules by choosing inversions. Guitarists inherit them, for better or worse, from the chord shapes.

  4. End with V–I. Use IV or ii before the V.
  5. Play it. Change anything your ear dislikes; try vi in place of a I, or ii in place of a IV.

Modulation: changing key

A secondary dominant visits another chord for a moment; that is tonicisation. When the music settles in the new key and confirms it with a cadence, it has modulated. Common methods:

  • Pivot chord. Use a chord that belongs to both keys as a hinge. C major and G major share C, Em, G and Am. The progression C – Am – D7 – G reads as I – vi in C, then the Am is reinterpreted as ii in G, followed by V7 – I in G.
  • Direct modulation. Simply begin the next phrase in the new key. The last chorus that jumps up a half step or whole step is the familiar example.
  • Common tone. Hold one note and change the harmony beneath it.
  • Sequence. Repeat a pattern at successively higher or lower levels until you arrive.

The nearest keys are the neighbours on the circle of fifths and the relative minor or major. Classical movements in a major key nearly always modulate to the dominant; in a minor key, to the relative major.

Chromatic chords of the classical style

  • Neapolitan (♭II). A major chord on the lowered second degree, usually in first inversion and in minor keys. In C minor: F–A♭–D♭, leading to G and then Cm.
  • Augmented sixth chords. Built on the lowered sixth degree with the raised fourth above it; both resolve outward by half step to the dominant. In C: A♭ and F♯ open out to G and G. Italian: A♭ C F♯. French: A♭ C D F♯. German: A♭ C E♭ F♯.
  • Diminished seventh. Because it is made entirely of minor thirds, any of its four notes can act as a leading tone. B°7, D°7, F°7 and A♭°7 are the same four pitches, so there are only three different diminished seventh chords, and each can resolve to four keys. Composers use it to modulate anywhere quickly.

Jazz harmony

  • ii–V–I everywhere. Jazz standards are chains of ii–V–I progressions in changing keys. In major: Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7. In minor: Dm7♭5 – G7♭9 – Cm.
  • Tritone substitution. G7 and D♭7 share the same tritone (B and F; in D♭7 they are spelled C♭ and F). Either can resolve to C. Dm7 – D♭7 – Cmaj7 gives a bass line that slides down by half steps.
  • Extensions and alterations. Dominant chords take 9, 13, ♭9, ♯9, ♯11 and ♭13 for colour. Major and minor sevenths take 9; minor takes 11; major takes ♯11.
  • Rootless voicings. The pianist leaves the root to the bass player and plays 3–5–7–9 or 7–9–3–5.
  • Chord–scale thinking. Each chord type suggests a scale to improvise with: maj7 → Ionian or Lydian; m7 → Dorian; 7 → Mixolydian; m7♭5 → Locrian; altered dominant → the altered scale (the seventh mode of melodic minor).

Other textures

  • Pedal point. A bass note held while the chords change above it. The opening of Bach’s C major Prelude holds C under a D minor chord in bar 2.
  • Ostinato. A short pattern repeated throughout, as in the left hand of Satie’s first Gymnopédie.
  • Quartal harmony. Chords stacked in fourths instead of thirds (D G C), open and ambiguous. Common in modal jazz.
  • Counterpoint. Two or more independent melodies at once. The four possible motions between two voices are parallel, similar, contrary and oblique (one voice stays still). Bach’s inventions and fugues are the school for it.

From motif to period

Music is built in levels, like language. A motif of a few notes is a word. Two or four bars make a phrase, a clause. Two phrases that balance each other make a period: the first, the antecedent, ends with a half cadence; the second, the consequent, begins the same way and ends with a full cadence. The first eight bars of “Ode to Joy” are a textbook period.

Write a piece in eight steps

  1. Choose a key and a metre. Start with C major or A minor, in 4/4 or 3/4.
  2. Choose a progression of four or eight bars from Chapter 12. Loop it in the progression player.
  3. Find a motif. Improvise over the loop, singing or playing, until two or three notes with a clear rhythm stick. Write them down.
  4. Make a phrase. State the motif, repeat it starting on a different note to fit the next chord, then round off the phrase. End the fourth bar on a note of the V chord: your question.
  5. Answer it. Begin the second phrase like the first; change the ending to arrive on the tonic over a V–I cadence.
  6. Write a contrasting section. Start on a different chord (IV or vi), use a different rhythm or register, and end on V so that the return of the opening feels inevitable.
  7. Return. Repeat the first section, perhaps with a fuller ending. You now have an A B A piece.
  8. Add a bass and a texture. Roots in the left hand or on the low strings at first; then try broken chords. Mark the dynamics. Play it for someone.

Your first twenty pieces will be imitations. Good. Every composer learned by copying models and changing one thing at a time. Finish each piece, however small, and write it down. An untidy finished piece teaches more than a brilliant fragment.

A short history

Around 1700 in Florence, Bartolomeo Cristofori built a harpsichord-shaped instrument in which the strings were struck by hammers instead of being plucked. Because the player could now control loudness by touch, it was called gravicembalo col piano e forte, “harpsichord with soft and loud”, soon shortened to pianoforte and then piano. Over the next two centuries it gained an iron frame, thicker strings at higher tension, a wider range and a faster action. The modern form was settled by the 1880s.

How it makes sound

  • Keys. 88 of them: 52 white and 36 black, from A0 (27.5 Hz) to C8 (4,186 Hz), a little over seven octaves.
  • Action. Each key is a lever. Pressing it throws a felt-covered hammer at the strings. Just before impact the hammer is released (the escapement), so it strikes freely and rebounds at once, leaving the string to vibrate. A grand’s repetition lever catches the hammer on its way down so the note can be struck again before the key has fully risen.
  • Dampers. A felt pad rests on each string and lifts when its key goes down. When you release the key the damper falls and stops the sound. The top octave and a half has no dampers; those short strings fade quickly on their own.
  • Strings. Steel wire. The lowest notes have one thick string wound with copper, the next group two, and from the tenor upward three strings per note tuned in unison.
  • Soundboard. A large, thin sheet of spruce under the strings. The strings pass over a wooden bridge glued to it; the board turns their vibration into sound in the air. The lid reflects that sound toward the audience.
  • Frame. A cast-iron plate that bears the combined pull of the strings.

Two consequences matter to the player. First, loudness depends only on how fast the hammer is travelling when it leaves the action, which means on how fast the key goes down. Pressing harder after the key has reached the bottom does nothing. Second, once a note has sounded you cannot swell it, bend it or add vibrato; you can only let it fade or stop it. A pianist shapes a line by grading the loudness of successive notes and by overlapping them.

Grand and upright

In an upright the strings and soundboard stand vertically and the hammers move horizontally, returning by springs. Repetition is a little slower and the touch lighter. The left pedal of an upright moves the hammers closer to the strings instead of shifting them, and the middle pedal usually lowers a strip of felt for quiet practice. A good upright is a fine instrument to learn on; so is a digital piano with weighted keys (Chapter 22). An unweighted keyboard will teach you the notes but not the touch.

How to sit

  1. Centre yourself opposite middle C, which lies near the maker’s name.
  2. Set the height of the bench so that, with your hands on the keys, your forearms are level with the floor or slope very slightly down toward the keys.
  3. Set the distance so your elbows hang a little in front of your body, slightly bent. Sit on the front half of the bench.
  4. Feet flat on the floor, right foot near the right pedal. Back tall, shoulders down.

Hand shape and finger numbers

Let your arm hang at your side. The loose curve your hand takes is the shape you bring to the keys. Fingers are gently curved and play on their tips; the thumb plays on the side of its tip; the knuckles form a low arch; the wrist is level and never locked. Nails must be short.

Both hands number the fingers from the thumb: thumb 1, index 2, middle 3, ring 4, little finger 5. Small numbers printed over notes are fingerings. They are advice from someone who has solved the passage already; follow them.

Sound should come from the weight of the arm released through a firm fingertip, with the finger staying close to the key. Lifting the fingers high and hammering wastes energy and makes an ugly tone. If your forearm or shoulder feels tight, stop, shake out, and start again more quietly.

Care

A piano should be tuned by a technician about twice a year and kept away from radiators, direct sun and outside doors; changes in humidity are what put it out of tune. Do not put drinks or plants on it.

The five-finger position

Moving the hand

Five fingers cover five notes. Music needs more, so the hand must travel. The two basic ways:

  • Shift: lift and place the whole hand in a new position, usually at the end of a phrase.
  • Extend or contract: open the hand so the fingers skip a key, as at the start of “Twinkle, Twinkle”, where the right hand plays C with the thumb and then G and A with fingers 4 and 5.

The third way, passing the thumb under the hand, belongs to scales and is in the next chapter.

Other positions

Move both hands up so that the thumbs and little fingers sit on G, and you are in G position (G A B C D). Everything you have learned transfers. “Mary Had a Little Lamb” in G position starts on B with finger 3 and has exactly the same fingering as in C. That is transposition by hand position.

Never practise a mistake. If a bar goes wrong twice, you are playing faster than you can think. Halve the tempo. Slow, correct repetitions are stored by the hands; fast, stumbling ones are stored too.

Scales and the thumb

A scale has eight notes and you have five fingers. The solution is to play the fingers in groups of three and four, with the thumb passing under the hand to begin each new group. Going up in the right hand, C major is fingered 1 2 3 – 1 2 3 4 – 5: after finger 3 plays E, the thumb slides under to F. Coming down, finger 3 crosses over the thumb. The left hand mirrors it: 5 4 3 2 1 – 3 2 1 going up, with finger 3 crossing over the thumb.

How to pass the thumb well: the moment the thumb has played, it begins to travel under the palm toward its next key, so that it arrives early and waiting. The hand stays level, the elbow does not swing out, and the note after the crossing is no louder than its neighbours. Practise C D E F as 1 2 3 1 over and over before you try the whole scale.

Touch

  • Legato: joined. Transfer the weight from finger to finger.
  • Staccato: detached. Release the key at once, from the finger for light staccato, from the wrist for a sharper one.
  • Non-legato: slightly separated; the default touch in much Baroque music.
  • Voicing: making one note of a chord, usually the top, louder than the rest. Lean the hand toward that finger and let it travel faster into the key.
  • Balance: melody louder than accompaniment. Practise by playing the melody forte and the accompaniment as nearly silent as you can.

The sustain pedal

Keep your heel on the floor and press the right pedal with the ball of the foot. Three techniques:

  • Direct pedalling. Pedal down with the chord, up before the next. Leaves a gap; used for rhythmic effects.
  • Legato (syncopated) pedalling. The essential skill. Play the new chord, and immediately after it sounds, lift the pedal and press it again. Hands and foot move at different moments: “play – up-down, play – up-down”. The old harmony is cleared and the new one caught without a gap or a blur.
  • Half pedalling. Raising the pedal only part of the way to thin out the resonance without losing all of it.

Change the pedal whenever the harmony changes, and whenever a melody moving by step in a low register starts to blur. Your ear is the judge.

Building speed safely

Speed comes from accuracy and relaxation, never from force. Use a metronome ladder: find the tempo at which a passage is perfect, play it three times, raise the metronome by four, repeat. When it breaks down, go back two steps and stop for the day. Pain is a signal to stop at once; tension in the forearm means you are pressing after the key is down.

1. Petzold: Minuet in G

What it teaches. Independence of the hands in two real melodic lines; reading a key signature (every F is F♯); three-four time.

How to practise. Play the eighth notes legato and the quarter notes slightly detached, as a dancer steps. Give the first beat of each bar a gentle lean. The left hand is a melody too: sing it.

How it is built. Sixteen bars, a period of two eight-bar phrases. Bars 1–8 end on the note A over a D in the bass: a half cadence on V. Bars 9–16 begin identically and end with a full cadence on G.

2. Bach: Prelude in C major

What it teaches. Evenness of touch; hearing harmony; memorising by chord.

How to practise. First play each bar as a single block chord, five notes held together, and listen to the progression. Only then break the chords. Keep the sixteenth notes perfectly equal and let the bass note ring.

How it is built. The chords are named above the staff. Bars 1–4 are I – ii – V – I over an almost stationary bass, a complete statement of the key. Bar 6 introduces F♯ in a D7 chord, the dominant of G, and bars 6–11 settle onto G: the piece has tonicised its dominant, exactly as Chapter 16 describes.

3. Beethoven: Für Elise

What it teaches. Passing a figure from hand to hand without a seam; a light touch; rotation of the forearm for the E–D♯ alternation.

How to practise. The opening alternation uses fingers 5 and 4 or 4 and 3; choose one and keep it. In bars 2–4 the left hand’s rising arpeggio hands over to the right hand’s three rising notes: practise until a listener could not tell where one hand stops. Use a touch of pedal on each left-hand arpeggio.

How it is built. Only two chords: A minor (i) and E major (V). The D♯ in the melody is a lower neighbour to E. The whole piece is a rondo, A B A C A, and this theme is the A.

4. Pachelbel: Canon in D

What it teaches. Playing over a ground bass; the most imitated chord progression in Western music; key of D (F♯ and C♯).

How it is built. D – A – Bm – F♯m – G – D – G – A: I – V – vi – iii – IV – I – IV – V. The bass falls a fourth, rises a step, and repeats that shape a third lower. The first melody descends by step through an entire octave of the D major scale. Try inventing a third variation of your own in eighth notes using notes of each chord.

5. Satie: Gymnopédie No. 1

What it teaches. Leaps in the left hand; pedalling; tone and patience at a very slow tempo; a floating melody over a quiet accompaniment.

How to practise. Learn the left-hand leap without looking: play the bass note, move, and touch the chord silently before sounding it. Change the pedal on every beat one. Play the melody with a flat, weighted finger and count every beat of the long notes.

How it is built. Two major seventh chords alternate: G–B–D–F♯ (Gmaj7, IV) and D–A–C♯–F♯ (Dmaj7, I). Neither is a dominant, so there is no pull, and the music seems to hang in the air. The F♯ on top is shared by both chords.

6. Beethoven: “Moonlight” Sonata

What it teaches. Even triplets at a slow tempo; holding a key signature of four sharps in your head; sustained pedalling; later in the movement, bringing out a melody with the little finger above the triplets.

How it is built. Bar 1: C♯ minor (i). Bar 2: the same chord with the bass stepping down to B. Bar 3: A major (VI), then D major with F♯ in the bass, which is the Neapolitan chord (♭II in first inversion) from Chapter 16. What a keyboard is

On a piano each key throws a hammer. On an electronic keyboard each key closes a pair of switches. The instrument measures how fast you pressed, looks up or calculates a sound, and sends it to loudspeakers or headphones. Because the sound is produced electronically, one set of keys can be a piano, an organ, a string section, a flute or a sound no acoustic instrument has ever made.

The keys

  • How many. A full piano has 88 keys. A 61-key instrument covers five octaves, C2 to C7, enough for nearly all popular music and for the early piano repertoire. Use the octave-shift buttons to reach the notes that are missing.
  • Action. Synth action: light, sprung, quick to return; ideal for organ and synth playing. Semi-weighted: some resistance. Hammer action: weighted to imitate a piano, heavier in the bass than the treble. If you intend to study piano, hammer action matters.
  • Touch sensitivity. A touch-sensitive (velocity-sensitive) keyboard plays louder when you strike faster. Very cheap instruments play every note at the same volume; avoid them, because you cannot learn dynamics on one.
  • Polyphony. The number of notes the instrument can sound at once, typically 32 to 256. A held pedal and a layered sound use it up quickly; when it runs out, the oldest notes are cut off.

MIDI

MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface, 1983) is a language of instructions. When you press middle C, the keyboard sends “note 60 on, velocity 92”; when you release it, “note 60 off”. It carries no sound. A MIDI recording is therefore a list of what you did, which can be edited note by note, played back with a different voice, slowed down without changing pitch, or printed as notation. Today MIDI usually travels over a USB cable to a computer, tablet or phone.

How electronic sound is made

Most keyboards play back samples, short recordings of real instruments, one or more per key. A synthesiser builds the sound instead. The classic design, subtractive synthesis, has three stages and you met its raw material in Chapter 1.

  1. Oscillator. Generates a simple repeating wave rich in harmonics: sawtooth (all harmonics, bright), square (odd harmonics, hollow), triangle (soft) or sine (pure).
  2. Filter. Removes harmonics. A low-pass filter cuts everything above its cutoff frequency; lower the cutoff and the sound grows darker. Resonance emphasises the harmonics near the cutoff.
  3. Amplifier with envelope. Shapes loudness over time in four stages, known as ADSR.
  • Attack: how long the sound takes to reach full level after the key goes down.
  • Decay: how long it takes to fall from that peak to the sustain level.
  • Sustain: the level held for as long as the key stays down. A level, not a time.
  • Release: how long the sound takes to die away after the key comes up.

Automatic accompaniment

An arranger keyboard can supply the band. You choose a style (pop, waltz, bossa nova, swing…) and a tempo. The keyboard is split: the keys below the split point, usually around the F♯ below middle C, become the chord zone. When you play a chord there, the instrument recognises it and generates drums, bass and chordal parts in that harmony until you play a different chord.   • Fingered mode: you play the actual chord, three or more notes in any inversion. Use this; it is real playing and it transfers to any keyboard.
  • Single-finger mode: one key gives a major chord, with extra keys for minor and seventh according to the maker’s scheme. Convenient at first, a dead end later.
  • Sync start: the band starts when you play the first chord.
  • Intro, fill, variation, ending: buttons that make the band play an introduction, a drum fill between sections, a busier pattern for the chorus and a proper ending.

The technique is in the timing. Change the left-hand chord exactly on the beat where the symbol stands, usually beat one, and a fraction early is better than late. You need not hold it down for the whole bar; most instruments remember the chord.

Arranging a song for yourself

  1. Voice. Choose one that suits the song and play in that instrument’s character (Chapter 22). A flute melody has breaths; a piano melody can have chords under it.
  2. Style and tempo. Match the metre first: a song in 3/4 needs a waltz style. Then the feel.
  3. Form. Intro, verse on the plain pattern, fill, chorus on the variation, ending. Write it on the lead sheet.
  4. Registration. Store the verse and chorus settings so that you change sound with one button, not six.

In a band

With a bass player and a guitarist, a keyboard player has to play less, not more.

  • Leave the low notes to the bass. Keep your left hand out of the bottom two octaves or drop it altogether.
  • Stay out of the guitar’s register and rhythm. If the guitar strums, hold a pad or play sparse chords higher up.
  • Use three-note voicings without the root.
  • Fill the gaps between the singer’s phrases; rest while the singer sings.

The transpose button and its trap

Transpose lets you play in C and sound in any key, which rescues you when a singer needs E♭. But if you use it for everything you will only ever know one key, your hands will not learn the black keys, and on a piano you will be lost. Use it as a tool for emergencies and learn at least the keys of C, G, D, F and B♭ properly.

The parts

  • Head with six tuning pegs. Turning a peg tightens or loosens a string.
  • Nut: the grooved strip where the head meets the neck. One end of the vibrating string.
  • Neck and fingerboard, crossed by metal frets. Dots at frets 3, 5, 7, 9 and a double dot at 12 help you find your place.

Strings and tuning

Strings are numbered from the thinnest. String 1 is the thin, high one nearest the floor; string 6 is the thick, low one nearest your face. Standard tuning from 6 to 1 is E A D G B E.

A common memory aid: Eddie Ate Dynamite, Good Bye Eddie. Neighbouring strings are a perfect fourth apart, except G to B, which is a major third. Remember that exception; it explains many shapes later.

Tuning

Tune every time you play. Strings drift with temperature and playing, and practising on an out-of-tune guitar trains your ear wrongly.

  • With a tuner. A clip-on tuner or phone app shows whether each string is flat (too low) or sharp (too high). Always tune up to the note: if you overshoot, loosen below it and come up again.
  • By the fifth-fret method. Get string 6 right from a reference. Press it at fret 5: that is A, and open string 5 should match. Fret 5 of string 5 gives open string 4; fret 5 of string 4 gives open string 3; fret 4 of string 3 gives open string 2; fret 5 of string 2 gives open string 1.
  • By beats. When two strings are almost at the same pitch you hear a slow pulsing. The pulse slows as they come together and vanishes when they agree.

How frets work

Pressing a string just behind a fret shortens its vibrating length, which raises its pitch. Each fret is one half step. Fret 12 is at exactly half the string’s length and gives the octave of the open string. The frets get closer together as you go up because each one shortens the remaining length by the same proportion, about 5.6%. This is Chapter 1 made of wood and wire.

Holding the guitar

  • Sitting, classical position: the waist of the guitar rests on the left thigh, the left foot raised on a footstool, the neck angled up. Best access to the whole neck.
  • Sitting, casual position: guitar on the right thigh, neck roughly level. Usual for steel-string and electric.
  • Either way: back straight, shoulders loose, the guitar held by the right forearm and your body, never by the left hand. The left hand must be free to move.

The two hands

Fretting hand (left, for a right-handed player). Fingers are numbered 1 index, 2 middle, 3 ring, 4 little finger. The thumb is not numbered; it rests behind the neck, roughly opposite the middle finger. Press with the fingertip, finger curved, immediately behind the fret, not in the middle of the space. That is where the least pressure gives a clean note. Keep the nails of this hand short.

Plucking hand. Fingerstyle players name the fingers from Spanish: p thumb, i index, m middle, a ring. The thumb usually takes strings 6, 5 and 4; i, m and a take strings 3, 2 and 1. With a pick (plectrum), hold it between the side of the index finger and the pad of the thumb with only a small point showing, and move from the wrist.

Notice that the guitar needs both hands to make one note: one chooses the pitch, the other sounds it. Co-ordinating the two is the first skill to build.

Tablature

Tablature, or TAB, is a picture of the strings. It has six lines. The top line is string 1, the thin one; the bottom line is string 6. A number on a line tells you which fret to press on that string; 0 means the open string. Numbers stacked vertically are played together.

TAB tells you where to play, which standard notation does not. It often does not tell you how long each note lasts. This book prints both together, so you get position from one and rhythm from the other. Learn to read both.

Chord boxes

A chord box is the neck seen from the front, standing upright. Vertical lines are strings, with string 6 on the left. Horizontal lines are frets, with the nut at the top. Dots show where to press and the numbers inside are fingers. Above the box, ○ means play the string open and × means do not play it. You will use them from the next chapter.

Your fingertips will hurt for the first two or three weeks. That is normal, and it passes as the skin hardens. Play for ten or fifteen minutes several times a day, not for one long session, and stop if a finger blisters. Do not press harder than you need: move closer to the fret instead.

How to learn any chord:

  1. Place the fingers one at a time, tips pressing straight down, just behind the frets.
  2. Pluck each string of the chord separately, low to high. Listen to every one.
  3. A buzz: move the finger nearer the fret, or press a little more. A dead string: some finger is leaning on it. Arch the fingers more so they clear the neighbouring strings.
  4. Take the hand away, shake it, and form the chord again. Repeat until the fingers land together.

The eight essential open chords

Look for families. E and A minor are the same shape on neighbouring sets of strings. E minor is E with one finger lifted. A minor and C share two fingers. D minor is D with one note lowered. Seeing these links halves the work of changing chords.

Every one of these shapes contains only the three notes of its triad, repeated in different octaves. Open C, low to high, is C E G C E: root, third, fifth, root, third. A guitar chord is a triad spread over five or six strings in whatever order the tuning allows.

Changing chords

The difficulty in guitar playing is rarely the chord. It is the change. Three techniques:

  • Anchor fingers. If a finger is on the same string and fret in both chords, leave it down. From C to A minor, fingers 1 and 2 stay; only finger 3 moves.
  • Guide fingers. If a finger stays on the same string but changes fret, slide it without lifting. From A to D, finger 3 slides from fret 2 to fret 3 on string 2.
  • Move as a shape. When nothing is shared, lift all the fingers together, form the new shape in the air, and land together. From G to C; from E minor to A minor.

The drill that works is the one-minute change: set a timer for one minute and count how many times you can switch cleanly between two chords, one strum each. Write the number down. Repeat daily. When you pass thirty, the change is ready for songs.

Strumming

Hold the pick lightly, or use the nail of the index finger. Let the forearm swing from the elbow with a loose wrist, as if shaking water off your hand.

  • Downstrokes (↓) fall on the beats: 1, 2, 3, 4.
  • Upstrokes (↑) fall on the “ands” between, and catch only the top three or four strings.
  • The hand never stops. It swings down–up–down–up in steady eighth notes whether or not it touches the strings. A rhythm is made by missing the strings on some swings, never by pausing the hand.

The pattern called the campfire strum here, ↓ · ↓ ↑ · ↑ ↓ ↑, fits a very large number of songs in 4/4. Say it as “down, down-up, up-down-up”, and keep the hand moving through the two silent swings.

Octave shapes

To find a note on another string, use these fixed shapes.

  • From string 6 or 5: the octave is two strings higher, two frets up. G at string 6 fret 3 → G at string 4 fret 5.
  • From string 4 or 3: the octave is two strings higher, three frets up. The extra fret makes up for the smaller gap between strings 3 and 2.
  • The same note name is found one string higher, five frets down (four when crossing from string 3 to 2), which is the fifth-fret tuning method run in reverse.

Barre chords

Place your first finger flat across all the strings at some fret and it becomes a movable nut. Any open chord shape formed above it with the other three fingers becomes a new chord. Two shapes do most of the work.

  • E shape. The open E chord moved up, with the barre behind it. Its root is on string 6. At fret 1 it is F, at fret 3 G, at fret 5 A.
  • A shape. The open A chord moved up. Its root is on string 5. At fret 1 it is B♭, at fret 2 B, at fret 3 C.

Lift or flatten a finger just as you would in the open shape to get the minor, seventh and minor seventh forms. With two shapes and the note names on two strings you can play any major or minor chord in at least two places.

Making a barre sound. Roll the first finger slightly onto its bony outer edge. Place it right behind the fret. Drop the wrist and bring the elbow in toward your side. Pull back lightly with the arm instead of squeezing with the thumb. Check one string at a time. Expect it to take some weeks; practise at fret 5 or 7 first, where the tension is lower, and work down to fret 1.

Power chords

Take only the lowest two or three strings of a barre chord: root and fifth, sometimes with the octave. This is the power chord, written with a 5 (G5). It has no third, so it is neither major nor minor, and it stays clear under distortion. It is the basis of rock rhythm guitar.

CAGED: five shapes for every chord

The five open major shapes C, A, G, E and D can each be moved up the neck with a barre, and in that order they link end to end. A C major chord can be played as the open C shape; as the A shape at fret 3; as the G shape at fret 5; as the E shape at fret 8; and as the D shape at fret 10. Then the cycle starts again an octave higher. The system is called CAGED. Its value is that every chord, arpeggio and scale has a home in each of the five regions, so you are never lost anywhere on the neck.

Picking

Alternate picking means strictly down, up, down, up, whatever the strings. It keeps single-note lines even and is the foundation of speed. Practise it on one string with a metronome, then on the pentatonic box from the last chapter.

Fingerstyle. The thumb (p) plays the bass strings 6, 5 and 4. The index (i), middle (m) and ring (a) fingers are assigned to strings 3, 2 and 1. Rest the hand lightly, fingers curled, and pluck by closing each finger toward the palm. In a free stroke the finger clears the next string; in a rest stroke it comes to rest against it, for a fuller, louder melody note.

Arpeggio study

This progression, Am–C–D–F and Am–C–E, is worth studying. In A minor it reads i–III–IV–VI and i–III–V. The D major chord (IV) contains F♯, borrowed from the Dorian mode or the melodic minor; the E major chord (V) contains G♯, the leading tone.

Slurs: hammer-ons, pull-offs and slides

  • Hammer-on (h in TAB). Pluck a note, then bring another finger down hard on a higher fret of the same string without plucking again.
  • Pull-off (p). The reverse: pluck a fretted note, then flick that finger off sideways so that it sounds a lower note already held or the open string.
  • Slide (/ or \). Pluck, then slide the finger along the string to another fret, keeping the pressure.

These are the guitar’s legato. They make lines smoother and faster, because the picking hand does half the work.

Bends and vibrato

Push a fretted string sideways across the fingerboard and its tension rises, and with it the pitch. A half bend raises the note by a half step, a full bend by a whole step. Support the bending finger with the fingers behind it and turn the wrist. Always bend to a target: play the destination note first, two frets higher, then match it with the bend. Vibrato is a small, rhythmic bend and release that makes a held note sing. No piano can do either. They are the reason the guitar can imitate a voice.

Muting and harmonics

  • Palm muting (P.M.). Rest the edge of the picking hand on the strings at the bridge for a short, chunky sound.
  • Left-hand muting. Relax the fretting fingers without lifting them to cut a chord short; let idle fingers touch strings that must not ring.
  • Natural harmonics. Touch a string lightly, directly above fret 12, 7 or 5, and pluck. You hear a clear, bell-like tone: the second, third or fourth harmonic of the open string (Chapter 1).

What they share

  • The same notes and the same theory. A C major chord is C, E and G on all three. Scales, intervals, keys, chord progressions and rhythm transfer completely. Everything in Parts I and II applies to each instrument without change.
  • They play harmony. All three can sound several notes at once, so each can accompany a singer or play alone. Most instruments, from the flute to the trumpet, cannot.
  • Piano and guitar are both string instruments. One strikes its strings with hammers, the other plucks them. In both, the note begins at full strength and fades.
  • Piano and keyboard share a layout. The pattern of black and white keys is identical, and so are the fingerings.

Range, to scale

One place or many

This is the deepest difference between the instruments, and the figure below lets you feel it.

On a keyboard, a pitch has one address. Reading is direct: one note on the page, one key. On a guitar, most pitches can be played in three or four places, each with a slightly different tone, and the player must choose a position before reading a note. The reward is that anything learned in one position can be moved elsewhere unchanged.

Keys or shapes

Because the keyboard has black and white keys in an irregular pattern, every scale and chord feels different under the hand. C major is all white; D major has two black keys; the fingering changes with them. A pianist learns twelve keys.

The guitar neck has no such landmarks: every fret is a half step and every position looks alike. A barre chord or a scale pattern moved two frets up is simply the same thing a whole step higher. A guitarist learns a few shapes and twelve places to put them. The price is that the guitar gives no visual clue to the names of the notes. Many guitarists play for years without knowing them, which is why Chapter 26 insists that you learn them.

The same chord on each

A pianist’s right hand plays a triad as three neighbouring notes inside one octave and can choose any inversion and spacing at will. The guitar’s tuning decides the voicing for you: open C is C–E–G–C–E across two octaves, with the root and the third doubled. That wide, ringing spread is the characteristic sound of the instrument. Close voicings that are trivial on a piano can be impossible on a guitar, and the reverse is true of some guitar voicings with open strings.

Sustain and expression

A piano note cannot be changed once struck, but the pedal lets dozens of notes ring together, so the piano excels at resonance, wide textures and many simultaneous lines. A guitar note fades quickly, but the player’s finger is on the string for its whole life, so the guitar excels at inflection: bends, slides and vibrato that imitate a voice. A keyboard can sustain a note for ever or bend it with a wheel, but only as far as the chosen voice and controls allow, and with less direct contact than either.

What only each can do

  • Piano: ten independent notes over seven octaves; a real bass line, inner harmony and melody together; enormous dynamic range from one player.
  • Keyboard: change instrument at the touch of a button; hold a note indefinitely; layer and split sounds; play with a rhythm section; record and edit what you played; practise silently.
  • Guitar: bend and shake the pitch; play the same note with different colours on different strings; strum; go anywhere.

Piano to keyboard

  • The keys are lighter. Play with less arm weight and more finger, or you will thump.
  • Sustained voices expose every careless release. Learn finger legato; forget the pedal on organ sounds.
  • Replace the written left-hand part with chord shapes, and learn to read lead sheets.
  • With auto-accompaniment, the machine keeps perfect time. You must follow it.

Keyboard to piano

  • The keys are heavier and respond to the smallest change of speed. Expect uneven tone at first; scales and Hanon at a slow tempo will cure it.
  • There is no band. The left hand has to become one: bass and harmony together (Chapter 20).
  • Learn to pedal. It is half of the piano’s sound.
  • Read the bass clef fluently, and stop relying on the transpose button.

Keys to guitar

  • Stop looking for the one right place. Choose a position and stay in it.
  • Your hands have unequal jobs now, and one note needs both. Go slowly until they synchronise.
  • Think in shapes, but name the notes as you play them, or your theory will stay behind on the keyboard.
  • You must tune. Your ear, trained on a fixed-pitch instrument, will have to become more exact.
  • The fingertips need weeks to toughen. Short daily sessions.

Guitar to keys

  • Every note has a name and one place; use that to finally learn where they all are.
  • Shapes no longer move. C major and D major are different under the hand; learn the common keys one at a time.
  • The left hand, used to the hard work, must now play a quiet accompaniment while the right hand leads.
  • Read the bass clef. Guitarists have never needed it.
  • Your chord knowledge transfers at once: you can accompany songs from chord symbols in the first week.

Playing together

  • Tune to the keyboard. A piano or keyboard cannot adjust; the guitar must match it. Take your A or E from the keys.
  • Agree the key with everyone in mind. Guitars are happiest in E, A, D, G and C, where open strings ring. Pianists are comfortable anywhere but tend toward C, F, G and B♭. Singers decide in the end; the guitarist’s capo and the keyboard’s transposer exist to serve them.
  • Share out the registers. Bass at the bottom, guitar in the middle, keyboard chords above it or a sustained pad behind, melody on top. If two instruments are in the same octave playing the same rhythm, one of them should change something.
  • Share out the rhythm. If the guitar strums eighth notes, the keyboard holds long chords. If the piano plays a busy pattern, the guitar plays sparse accents.
  • Remember the octave. Guitar music sounds an octave lower than written. A guitarist reading from a piano score will be an octave below the pianist playing the same line.

Feel how 1, 3 and 5 rest, and how 4 leans down and 7 leans up. Indian classical training is built on this practice, and it is the most efficient ear exercise there is.

Scale degrees: hearing a note’s job

Hearing which degree of the key a note is turns out to be more useful than hearing isolated intervals, because melodies are heard in a key. The trainer below first plays a short cadence, I–IV–V–I, to fix the key in your ear, then one note. Name its degree.

Intervals

Work in this order: octave and fifth; major and minor third; fourth; major and minor second; sixths; sevenths; tritone. When two intervals confuse you, practise only those two until the difference is obvious.

Rhythm

Rhythmic ear training is tapping back what you hear. Use the tap trainers in Chapters 3 and 5 with your eyes closed: listen to the playback of a rhythm, then start the count-in and reproduce it from memory.

Taking down a tune by ear

Transcription is the complete exercise. Start with a nursery rhyme, then a song you know well.

  1. Find the tonic. Hum the note on which the tune could end and feel finished. Find it on your instrument. That gives you the key.
  2. Find the first note as a scale degree. Most tunes start on 1, 3 or 5.
  3. Follow the contour. Step or leap? Up or down? Steps are the next scale note. For a leap, sing up the scale between the two notes and count.
  4. Write the rhythm separately: tap the beat, find where the long notes fall.
  5. Find the bass notes, then the chords. The lowest note at each chord change is usually the root. Try I, IV, V and vi before anything else.
  6. Check by playing along with the recording.

Relative pitch, the ability to hear how notes relate to each other and to a key, is what musicians need, and anyone can learn it. Absolute or “perfect” pitch, naming a note with no reference, is rare, mostly acquired in early childhood, and not required for anything in music.

Start with five notes

The minor pentatonic scale has no half steps and no note that clashes badly with the chords of its key. That makes it the safest place to begin. Over any progression in C major or A minor, use A minor pentatonic: A C D E G.

Six rules that make random notes into music

  1. Play less. Begin with one note and vary only its rhythm. Add a second note after a minute. Restraint sounds like confidence.
  2. Leave space. Play a short phrase, then rest for as long as the phrase lasted. Wind players must breathe; imitate them.
  3. Repeat yourself. Play an idea, then play it again. Then change its last note. Repetition is what makes a listener hear an idea as intended.
  4. Question and answer. Let one phrase end in the air, on a note other than the tonic; let the next end on the tonic.
  5. Aim for chord tones. Land on a note of the current chord on the strong beats, especially when the chord changes. The notes in between are free.
  6. Carry on. A wrong note is a half step from a right one. Slide to the neighbour and it becomes an ornament.

Developing a motif

Take three notes, say A–C–D. You can repeat them; start them on a different beat; play them backwards; stretch the rhythm to twice the length; move the shape up to the next notes of the scale (C–D–E, D–E–G), which is a sequence; or change one note. An entire solo can grow from one small cell handled this way, and it will sound coherent because the listener keeps recognising it.

The blues

The twelve-bar blues is the classic school of improvisation: a form short enough to memorise, slow enough to think in, and forgiving of the blues scale throughout.

A plan for your first choruses:

  • Chorus 1: one note only, the tonic A, in different rhythms.
  • Chorus 2: three notes, A C D. Use the blues lyric shape: a phrase over bars 1–4, the same phrase over bars 5–8, an answering phrase over bars 9–12.
  • Chorus 3: the whole scale. Put the highest note in bar 9 or 10.
  • Guitarists: bend the D on string 3 up toward E; slide into notes from a fret below.
  • Pianists: play the scale in the right hand over left-hand shells: A–G for A7, D–C for D7, E–D for E7. Crush the C into C♯ over the A7 chord: that clash of minor third against major third is the sound of blues piano.

Beyond the pentatonic

  • Major pentatonic (1 2 3 5 6) for bright, country and gospel colours over major chords.
  • The full major scale with care over the fourth degree, which rubs against the I chord.
  • Modes (Chapter 14): Dorian over a minor chord vamp, Mixolydian over a dominant chord.
  • Arpeggios: outline each chord with its own notes. This is how jazz players make the harmony audible in a single line.
  • Guide tones: in a ii–V–I, follow the thirds and sevenths as they step from chord to chord (Chapter 13) and decorate that line.

Accompanying by ear

To accompany a song you have never seen written down:

  1. Find the key by finding the note the tune rests on.
  2. Write down I, IV, V and vi for that key. A large majority of popular songs use only these.
  3. Start on I. When the tune stops fitting, change: try V, then IV, then vi. Your ear will tell you at once.
  4. Note where the changes fall; they almost always come on beat one, and the pattern usually repeats every four or eight bars.
  5. Choose a texture from Chapter 20, 23 or 25 to match the song.

If the key does not suit the singer, move it: think in numerals, then name the chords in the new key (Chapter 12), or use a capo or the transposer.

  • stop and start the phrase again
  • play it louder

Principles

  • Little and often. Twenty minutes every day beats three hours on Sunday. Skills are consolidated during sleep, so each day counts once.
  • One clear goal per session. “Play bars 9 to 12 hands together at 60 bpm, three times clean” is a goal. “Practise the piece” is not.
  • Slow enough to be right. You are programming your hands. They store whatever you repeat, including mistakes. Choose a tempo at which you can play correctly, however slow.
  • Small pieces. Work on the smallest unit that contains the problem: a bar, a beat, two notes. Then join it to its neighbours, overlapping by a note.
  • Separate, then combine. Hands separately on keyboards. On guitar, the fretting-hand shapes without plucking, then the plucking pattern on open strings.
  • Count and use the metronome. Find the tempo where a passage is secure, raise it four clicks at a time, and stop when it breaks.
  • Five in a row. A passage is learned when you can play it correctly five times consecutively. If you miss on the fifth, the count returns to zero.
  • Start anywhere. Practise beginning from the middle and the end of a piece, so the last page is as strong as the first.
  • Record yourself. Once a week. You cannot listen fully while you are busy playing.
  • Stop while it is going well. End on a correct repetition, and before fatigue brings errors.

Your body

Playing should never hurt. Aching fingertips on a new guitar are the exception and pass in weeks. Pain in the wrist, forearm, thumb or shoulder means tension or a bad position: stop, shake out, check your posture against Chapters 18 and 24, and resume more softly and slowly. Take a short break every twenty-five minutes. If pain persists, rest for some days and see a doctor before it becomes an injury.

Performing

Nerves are normal; preparation is the remedy. A piece is ready to perform when you can play it three times in a row without a serious slip, start from any phrase, and get through it at a slower tempo than usual (which tests whether you know it or only your fingers do). Play for one friend before you play for ten. If you slip in performance, keep the beat going and rejoin at the next bar; the audience follows the rhythm far more closely than the notes.
